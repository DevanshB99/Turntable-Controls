What changed

Drag a handle on a plot, or move a slider. This panel explains what it did and why the three plots moved together.

    Two curves and one rule. Everything else on this page is a consequence of them. Find where the magnitude curve crosses 0 dB, then look straight down at the phase curve at that same frequency. How far the phase sits above −180° there is your entire safety margin.

    The rule

    Why −180° is the cliff edge

    Negative feedback works by subtracting the measurement from the target. That subtraction only helps if the measurement arrives roughly on time.

    Now suppose a wobble travels round the loop and comes back half a cycle late — that is what −180° of phase means. Half a cycle late is upside down. The subtraction now behaves like an addition, and the correction arrives exactly in time to make the wobble bigger.

    If the loop is also amplifying at that frequency — magnitude above 0 dB — the wobble grows on every lap. That is instability, and it is nothing more mysterious than the geometry of two curves.

    So you need either less than 0 dB of gain, or more than −180° of phase, at every single frequency. Trouble only lives where they overlap.

    Reading magnitude

    What the height of the curve tells you

    Magnitude is how much the loop amplifies a disturbance at each speed of wiggle. It is drawn in decibels, so 0 dB means "same size out as in", +20 dB means ten times bigger, −20 dB means ten times smaller.

    On the left — low frequency — you want the curve high. That is slow, persistent stuff: a steady friction load, a heavy part on one side of the disc. High gain there means the loop crushes those errors.

    On the right — high frequency — you want the curve low. That is fast, twitchy stuff: encoder noise, the last digit flickering. Low gain there means the loop ignores it instead of chasing it.

    The crossover is where you hand over from one behaviour to the other. Choosing it is most of what tuning is.

    Reading phase

    Where the lag comes from

    Every phase curve here is the sum of a few fixed contributions, and it helps to know which part is which:

    SourcePhase it costs
    The motor itself (position is the integral of step rate)−90° everywhere
    Actuator lag τa0 → −90°
    Encoder filter τf0 → −90°
    Dead time Td−ω·Td, no limit

    The first three level off. Dead time never does — it keeps taking phase for ever as frequency rises. That is why it, and not the motor, is what ultimately limits how fast this machine can be.

    Term by term

    What each gain does to the two curves

    Kp moves magnitude only. Doubling it lifts the whole magnitude curve by 6 dB and leaves the phase curve untouched — not approximately, exactly. But lifting the curve slides the crossover to the right, and since phase falls as frequency rises, you end up reading the phase curve at a worse place. Kp costs you margin without ever touching phase.

    Ki lifts the left-hand end and bends phase down toward −90° there. Below the corner at ω = Ki/Kp it dominates; above it, it is invisible.

    Kd lifts the right-hand end and pushes phase back up near its corner at ω = Kp/Kd. It is the only term that gives margin back — and the only one that amplifies your encoder's noise.

    Drag the pucks on the Bench tab and watch each of these happen.

    The pole map

    Reading the unit circle

    The Bode plots describe the loop before you close it. The pole map describes the machine after you close it — the actual behaviour you will feel.

    Because the controller runs at a fixed 50 Hz (every 20 ms on the ESP32), each pole is a number that the error gets multiplied by on every tick. Inside the circle, that number is less than one, so disturbances shrink. Outside, they grow.

    Distance from the centre tells you speed: closer to the edge is slower. Angle tells you ringing: a pole sitting on the positive real axis never oscillates no matter how slow it is, while a pole at a steep angle rings. That is why a pole at 0.99 on the axis is fine and a pole at 0.90 at 45° is not.

    The limit of this model

    What the plots cannot see

    Tick Include hardware limits and the response changes while the Bode plots do not move at all. They have not failed — they were never able to see it.

    Bode analysis assumes the system is linear. A clamp on the output, a deadband near the target, and an encoder that reports whole counts are all non-linear. None of them can be drawn as a curve.

    The practical version: if the command trace sits pinned at its limit for most of the move, the loop is effectively open and every number on this page is describing a machine you are not currently running. Gains applied to a saturated actuator do nothing.

    A method, in order. The point of loop shaping is that every move you make has a reason you can say out loud — which is the whole difference between this and turning knobs until it looks better.

    Order of operations

    Five steps, and none of them skip

    1Kp alone, first Set Ki and Kd to zero. Raise Kp until the phase margin readout lands in the 45–65° band. Watch the crossover walk right as you do — that is your loop getting faster, and speed is the only thing Kp buys.
    2Stop at the red line The dead-time ceiling is a wall, not a guideline. If crossover goes past it the phase has already fallen through −180° and no amount of I or D will rescue you. Getting past it needs a faster loop or less delay, which is a hardware job.
    3Add Ki only for a standing error Watch the end of the response. If it parks short of the target and just sits there, that is friction the proportional term is too weak to break, and integral is the cure. If it reaches the target on its own, leave Ki at zero.
    4Add Kd only if you need margin you cannot get another way Raise it and watch the phase curve lift near crossover. Then look at what it did to the right-hand end of the magnitude curve — that is your encoder noise being amplified, and it is the price.
    5Confirm on hardware Turn on the hardware limits here first to see what saturation will do. Then run the real machine and check the response against the acceptance list.

If you have ever tested a controller by doubling a gain to see what happens, you were measuring gain margin without knowing it had a name.

    On the machine

    Tests the simulation cannot run for you

    The scaling test. Run 10°, 45°, 90° and 180° moves and plot each one normalised to its own target. A linear loop gives four curves that lie almost on top of each other. If instead the rise time grows in proportion to the distance, the actuator is saturating and running at one fixed speed — and none of the tuning on this page applies until that is fixed.

    The disturbance test. Let the turntable settle, then nudge it about 10° by hand and let go. It should come back promptly and with the same character as a step response. Sluggish return means Kp is too low. Oscillating return means too high. No return at all means friction is beating a weak Kp.

    The repeatability test. Run the same move five times. If the numbers move by more than about 10%, something in the system is not deterministic — usually latency piling up — and you are tuning against noise.

    A fair question

    Why not root locus?

    Root locus draws where the closed-loop poles travel as one gain varies. It is elegant and it is on every controls syllabus. It also needs the plant to be a ratio of polynomials.

    This plant has a pure delay in it. A delay is not a polynomial and never will be — written honestly it has infinitely many poles. To draw a root locus you have to approximate the delay away first, and the delay is the thing that is limiting you. You would be discarding the answer in order to make the method work.

    On a Bode plot the same delay is trivial: magnitude completely untouched, phase −ω·Td. Drag the Td slider and watch — the magnitude curve does not move at all while the phase curve pivots downward. Loop shaping handles delay exactly, which is why it is the right tool for this machine.

    Damping is the shape knob, and it is separate from the speed knob. ζ does not say how fast the turntable gets there — it says what it does when it arrives: sail past and come back, or ease in and stop.

    ζ = 0 · never stops 0.3 · rings 0.7 · the usual answer 1.0 · no overshoot >1 · crawls

    The bottom of the range

    ζ = 0 — what it means and when to use it

    What it is. No damping whatsoever. Energy that goes into the system never leaves. Push the turntable and it oscillates around the target for ever at exactly constant amplitude — never growing, never dying.

    On this page, setting ζ = 0 puts the target line exactly on −180°, which is to say it asks for zero phase margin. Those are the same statement. The pole map shows it as poles sitting exactly on the unit circle: they multiply the error by exactly 1 every tick.

    When to use it as a design target: never. A machine at ζ = 0 is not slow or ugly, it is unusable — it never finishes a move. And it is not a safe place to sit, because any small change (a warmer bearing, a tighter belt, slightly more delay) pushes it to the unstable side.

    When it is genuinely useful: as a reference line, and as an experiment. Set it to 0 once to see exactly where the edge is — the line you must not let the phase curve touch at crossover. And there is one classic tuning method that deliberately drives the real machine to constant oscillation, measures the gain and the period at which it happens, and computes gains from those two numbers. If you ever run that experiment, ζ = 0 is the condition you are hunting for — with a hand on the stop button.

    The top of the range

    ζ = 1 — what it means and when to use it

    What it is. Critically damped: the exact boundary between overshooting and not. Zero overshoot, and the fastest possible response that still has zero overshoot. The turntable eases into the target and stops dead — it never passes it, not even slightly.

    Anything above ζ = 1 is overdamped. Still no overshoot, but now slower for no benefit — the response crawls in. There is no reason to go past 1 on purpose.

    When to use it. Whenever passing the target is worse than arriving late:

    • Imaging. If the camera triggers on arrival, an overshoot means the part is still moving back when the shutter opens. That is a blurred or mis-registered image, and no amount of processing recovers it.
    • Anything the disc can collide with — a fixture, a probe, a light ring. Overshoot is a physical impact.
    • Backlash. Overshooting means reversing direction, and every reversal takes up belt slack in the other direction. A move that only ever approaches from one side is repeatable in a way that one which overshoots is not.

    The cost is honest: about 30–40% longer to settle than ζ = 0.7. If your cycle time can absorb that, ζ = 1 is the safer machine.

    In between

    Why 0.7 is the default answer

    ζ ≈ 0.707 is the value that reaches the ±2% band soonest. Less damping is quicker to get near the target but then wastes time ringing; more damping never rings but takes longer to arrive. The minimum sits at about 0.7, and it costs roughly 4% overshoot.

    ζOvershootPhase marginCharacter

    The phase-margin column is why this page can talk about ζ at all: asking for a damping ratio is the same as asking for a phase margin. A rough shortcut worth remembering is that phase margin in degrees ≈ 100 × ζ, up to about ζ = 0.6.

    Reading it back

    Asked versus achieved

    The ζ slider sets a target. The Achieved ζ readout is different — it is measured from where the closed-loop poles actually landed, so it tells you what the machine is really doing.

    When achieved is below your target, the loop is livelier than you asked for: expect more overshoot than the table predicts. Lower Kp, or add Kd to lift the phase at crossover.

    When achieved is above target, you are safer than you asked to be — which sounds good but means you are leaving speed unused. Raise Kp and watch the crossover move right.

    One honest caveat. The overshoot-versus-ζ table above is exact for a system with exactly two poles. This machine has more than two, plus a delay, so once ζ drops below about 0.4 the table starts over-predicting the overshoot you actually measure. In the range you should be working in, it is close.

    Four numbers describe your machine. They are not tuning knobs — you do not get to choose them, you measure them. Until they match your real rig, every curve on the Bench tab is describing somebody else's turntable.

    The model

    How the four fit together

    command ──▶ [ actuator ] ──▶ [ integrate ] ──▶ angle Ka, τa speed→position │ measured angle ◀── [ delay ] ◀── [ filter ]│ Td τf ▼

    Read it as a sentence: a command becomes a speed (that is Ka, and it takes τa seconds to get there), speed accumulates into position (that is the turntable itself), and the angle comes back to you smoothed and late (that is τf and Td).

    The "integrate" box is worth pausing on, because it is free and it is the reason this machine is more forgiving than most. You command a speed but you care about a position, and position is just speed added up over time. That built-in accumulation is why a plain proportional controller can reach the target here with no steady-state error at all — the plant is doing the integrating for you.

    Constant 1 of 4

    Ka — actuator gain

    What it is. How many degrees per second the shaft (or disc) actually turns for 1 microstep/s of command. Units: °/s per step/s.

    Easiest: take it from system ID. The scripts fit sys_p = K/(s+p), and Ka = K. The bare NEMA17 gave K = 0.1235. Theory at 1/16 microstepping is 360/3200 = 0.1125, so the fit is about 10% high. For the turntable, theory is 360/(3200×4) = 0.0281 until System_ID_Turntable.m gives you the real number. The pole p (0.008 rad/s) is so slow it is effectively a pure integrator, which is what this page assumes.

    To check it by hand.

    1. Turn the controller off so nothing is closing the loop. You are driving the motor open-loop.
    2. Send one fixed command value and leave it running.
    3. Log the encoder angle for 10 seconds. Count the full revolutions and add the leftover, so you have total degrees turned — not just the final reading, which wraps.
    4. Ka = (degrees turned ÷ seconds) ÷ command value.
    5. Repeat at three different command values, say a slow, a medium and a fast one.

    Step 5 is the important one. If you get the same Ka all three times, your actuator is linear and everything on this page is valid. If Ka changes with command size, stop — that is the bug to fix, and no tuning will work until it is.

    Why it matters. Ka multiplies loop gain in exactly the same way Kp does. Doubling Ka and doubling Kp are indistinguishable to the loop. Try it: set Ka to 2 and halve Kp, and every curve on the Bench tab stays put.

    What changes if it changes. The magnitude curve slides up or down bodily; phase does not move. So a belt that starts slipping quietly looks exactly like someone turned Kp down.

    Constant 2 of 4

    Td — dead time

    What it is. The flat delay between the motor moving and the controller seeing it. Your PID runs on the ESP32, so USB is not inside the loop. What remains is the MT6701 library: it reads the encoder in a background task every 10 ms, so the angle the loop uses is 0–10 ms old, about 5 ms on average. Units: seconds.

    How to measure it.

    1. Start from rest and make sure the angle reading is completely still.
    2. Send a large step command and record the timestamp at the moment you send it.
    3. Scan forward through the logged angles for the first sample that differs from rest by more than one encoder count.
    4. Td is the gap between those two timestamps.
    5. Do it about 20 times and take the median, not the average — one unlucky run with a long buffer stall would drag a mean badly.

    Why it matters — this is the most important of the four. Dead time is pure loss. It costs phase at −ω·Td and gives back nothing, and unlike every other lag here it never levels off. It sets a hard ceiling on how fast this machine can ever be:

    crossover ω ≲ 0.5 / Td

    On this page the ceiling uses Td plus half a sample (10 ms), because holding each command for one 20 ms tick behaves like that much extra delay. With Td = 5 ms that is about 33 rad/s. Kp ≈ 70 puts crossover near 9 rad/s, comfortably below it; the pidTuner gains Kp = 236 and 295 put it near 30 and 37 rad/s, right up against it. So on this setup the 20 ms loop rate is the main limit, more than the encoder delay. (Your 33–55% overshoots in July were measured before anti-windup was added; this page reproduces them only with anti-windup switched off, so windup at the clamp caused them.) That is a property of your firmware, not of your tuning. No value of Kp, Ki or Kd beats it. Chasing past it is the single most common way to waste weeks on a controller.

    What changes if it changes. Drag the Td slider and watch carefully: the magnitude curve does not move at all, while the phase curve pivots downward and the red ceiling line slides. Halving your dead time is worth more than any gain you will ever find.

    Constant 3 of 4

    τa — actuator lag

    What it is. How long the motor takes to actually reach a speed you asked for. Your firmware has no acceleration ramp: it changes the step interval immediately. So τa is only the real inertia of the rotor, plus the belt and disc on the turntable. On the bare motor it is too small for the 20 ms system ID to see, so a few milliseconds is a fair guess. Units: seconds.

    How to measure it.

    1. Controller off again, open loop.
    2. From a standstill, command a step change to a steady speed and log the angle at your fastest sample rate.
    3. Convert angle to speed by taking the difference between consecutive samples and dividing by the time between them.
    4. Find the final steady speed, then find the moment the speed first reaches 63% of it. The time from the command to that moment is τa.

    Why 63%? That is what a first-order lag does in exactly one time constant. It is not an arbitrary threshold — it is the definition.

    From system ID instead. If the 2-pole fit (sys2) on the turntable clearly beats the 1-pole fit, its faster pole p₂ gives τa = 1/p₂. If the two fits are equal, τa is too small to matter.

    What changes if it changes. τa bends the magnitude curve steeper above ω = 1/τa and costs up to 90° of phase there. A gentler acceleration ramp is kinder to the belt but directly buys you a slower loop — that is a real trade, not a free choice.

    Constant 4 of 4

    τf — encoder filter

    What it is. The smoothing applied to the raw angle before it is used. It exists because the encoder can only report whole counts, so a perfectly still turntable still flickers between two adjacent values. Units: seconds.

    On your firmware it is 0: the angle goes into the PID unfiltered. Only the derivative is filtered, and that filter is built into this page. If you ever add an angle filter that blends each new reading with weight α on a loop that runs every Tloop seconds:

    τf = T_loop × (1 − α) / α

    The same formula gives your derivative filter: α = 0.2 on the 20 ms loop is 0.02 × 0.8 / 0.2 = 0.08 s.

    How to check it is right. Hold the turntable still and log the angle for 30 seconds. The spread of the readings tells you how much noise is getting through. Then run a fast move and see how far the filtered reading lags the real one. Those two measurements are the two ends of the same trade.

    Why it matters, and the trade you cannot escape. A filter reduces noise by averaging over time, and averaging over time means the reading describes where the disc was, not where it is. There is no setting that gives you both. Turn τf up and the response gets visibly smoother while the phase margin readout falls.

    What changes if you remove it. Set τf to 0 and the phase curve lifts slightly — you get margin back. You also feed raw quantisation noise straight into the controller, which matters enormously if Kd is non-zero and barely at all if it is not.

    Doing it properly

    One experiment that gives you three of them

    You already do this: System_ID_Nema17_TMC.m and System_ID_Turntable.m drive the motor open-loop with V commands and log the angle. Ka is K from the fit. If you want τa as well, a single well-logged open-loop step does it by hand:

    1. Log time, command and angle together, at the fastest rate you can manage.
    2. From rest, step the command to a fixed value and hold it for several seconds.
    3. Plot the angle against time. Then plot the speed against time.

    From that one speed plot: the flat part before anything happens is Td. The final steady speed divided by your command is Ka. The time from the start of movement to 63% of that final speed is τa.

    Type the three numbers into the sliders on the Bench tab and the model becomes a model of your machine. Everything it then tells you is about your rig rather than a plausible example.

    Read this before using the table

    Why classic ZN fails on this machine

    Ziegler and Nichols fitted their rules in 1942 to self-regulating processes — a tank, a furnace, a heat exchanger. Give one of those a step and it settles at a new value on its own:

    G(s) = K e^(-Ls) / (Ts + 1) settles by itself

    Your turntable does not do that. Command a speed and the angle ramps forever. There is a free integrator in the plant:

    G(s) = Ka e^(-Td s) / [ s (τa s+1)(τf s+1) ] never settles

    That difference is fatal for the classic table. ZN sets the integral time to Ti = Tu/2, which puts integral action right at the crossover frequency. Add that to the integrator the plant already has, and near crossover you have two integrators in series — −180° of phase from the 1/s² alone, before the dead time has taken anything.

    The table above will show you the consequence: the classic rule lands a closed-loop pole outside the unit circle.

    Measuring it for real

    Don't push the gain up — use a relay

    The textbook way to find Ku is to raise Kp until the machine oscillates. On a motor or belt-driven disc under a camera that means deliberately destabilising it, with the swing limited only by your output clamp.

    There is a safer method that gets the same two numbers. Replace the controller with a relay: command a fixed speed one way while the disc is short of target, the other way when it is past. The loop self-oscillates at exactly the frequency you are looking for, and the amplitude is bounded by the relay size you chose.

    u = +d when error > +h u = −d when error < −h T_u = measured period K_u = 4d / (π √(a² − h²)) a = amplitude of the swing

    Keep d small, a couple of hundred steps/s, and the swing stays around a degree and never runs away. The hysteresis h keeps encoder flicker from chattering the relay; a few MT6701 counts (about 0.05°) is right.

    Expect this

    The relay is approximate, and that is fine

    The Ku formula assumes the swing is a sine wave. Feed a square wave into an integrator and you get a triangle, whose fundamental is 8/π² = 0.81 of the peak — so Ku reads about 19% low. The hysteresis adds a second bias, shifting the oscillation to where the phase is −180° + arcsin(h/a) rather than exactly −180°.

    Simulated on this plant, the relay recovers Tu within about 20% and Ku within about 30%. Bigger relay amplitude reduces both biases, at the cost of a bigger swing.

    So use the relay to check the model, not to set the gains. A 30% error in Ku propagates straight into every ZN formula. But if hardware and this page agree on Tu to within 20%, the model is describing your real machine — and then every other number the Bench tab gives you is trustworthy.

    The honest recommendation

    What to do instead

    Run the relay experiment. Compare Tu against the value at the top of this page. If they agree, you have validated the model — which is worth far more than a gain triple, because it means the crossover, phase margin and dead-time ceiling on the Bench tab all describe your actual hardware.

    Then tune by loop shaping, not by table. The reason is visible in the table above: the published rules are one-size-fits-all approximations fitted to a plant that is not yours, while loop shaping uses your measured constants directly.

    If you want an automatic rule that is at least designed for an integrating plant, use SIMC rather than ZN — it is in the table, and it is stable. It will still be more oscillatory than a hand-shaped loop, because it is tuned for disturbance rejection on a process plant rather than for a clean point-to-point move.
